What usually goes wrong on a logistics site

The person searching has a specific problem: a route, a product type, a volume, or a warehouse that has let them down.

Flexible solutions tailored to your needs

Every competitor says it. It qualifies nobody and it ranks for nothing, because nobody searches for a phrase that means nothing.

Minimums are hidden until the call

A business shipping two hundred units a month and one shipping fifty thousand need different providers. Hiding the threshold wastes their time and yours.

Integrations are the deciding factor and are not listed

Shopify, WooCommerce, Amazon, a specific ERP. If you plug into it, that belongs on a page. It is often the whole reason a shortlist gets made.

One page for every sector you serve

Cold chain, hazardous goods, ecommerce fulfilment and pallet freight are different searches with different buyers. A single services page competes for none of them.

What I would change

Make the qualifying facts findable, and let the wrong enquiries rule themselves out.

A page per service and per sector

Fulfilment, freight, warehousing, returns, each as its own page, and the sectors you specialise in likewise. That is how the searches are actually phrased.

Minimums and volumes stated

The volumes you want and the ones you cannot serve well. Saying so attracts better fitting enquiries and removes the ones that waste a sales call.

Integrations listed by name

Every platform and system you connect to, named. This is frequently the single fact that puts you on a shortlist.

Routes and coverage made concrete

Countries, lanes, hubs and what happens at customs. Since Brexit this is a live question for anyone shipping into or out of the UK.

Proof that you are reliable

On time percentages, capacity, accreditations. Reliability is the entire product and it is usually asserted rather than evidenced.

An enquiry path for a logistics manager

Short, specific, answerable from what they already know, and usable on a phone from a warehouse floor.

Fair questions

Our business is all relationships and tenders. Does search matter?

Tenders still start with someone finding candidates, and relationships still check the website first. Search rarely wins the contract; it decides whether you are on the list.

We do not want small enquiries. Will this bring more of them?

The opposite, if it is done properly. Publishing your minimums is the cheapest filter there is, and it means the enquiries that do arrive are worth answering.

Can you handle a site with hundreds of location pages?

Yes, but I would question whether you need hundreds. Thin location pages that differ by one town name tend to be a liability rather than an asset.

How does this work with an existing tender process?

The site's job is to get you into consideration and to answer the qualifying questions before a human has to. The tender process itself is yours.
